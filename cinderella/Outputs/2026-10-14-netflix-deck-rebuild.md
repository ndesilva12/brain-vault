a meeting with **Brandon Riegg (VP, Unscripted & Documentary Series)** and/or **Gabe Spitzer (VP,

## 1. The diagnosis, in one line

> **This is an investor deck wearing a buyer's cover.**

The design is genuinely good — better than most things that cross a Netflix desk. The problem is
architectural: **five of the sixteen slides are written for a celebrity or an LP**, and the
questions a head of sports actually asks are not answered anywhere in the document.

The deck tells Netflix **what the business is**. It never tells them **what the show is**.

## 6. ⚠️ The two things you must decide before the meeting

**1. Who is the producer, and are they in the room?**
Netflix does not buy from first-time producers with a deck. They buy from **Boardwalk, Words +
Pictures, Skydance, Religion of Sports.** Per `Process/2026-09-24-boardwalk-pictures-call.md`,
Andrew Fried brought his CFO to a first creative call — that is a buying signal, and **Boardwalk
made both of your comps.**

⚠️ **The Last Chance U paradox is real and it is specific to Netflix:** Boardwalk owning LCU proves
the genre works with this producer, *and* gives Netflix a reason to feel it already did college
basketball. Prepare the answer rather than hoping it does not come up. **The answer is on slide 10.**

**Recommendation: do not take the Netflix meeting without a producer attached**, or at minimum
without being able to say "Boardwalk is in advanced discussions and will produce." A head of sports
hearing "we haven't picked a producer" files you under development risk.

**2. Final cut.**
Netflix will want it. Your Loeb §H23 principle requires **compliance counsel to read the cut**
before release. These collide, and it will surface in the deal, not the pitch. Decide now whether
your position is a compliance-only review right (narrow, defensible, Netflix will probably live
with it) or something broader (they will not). **Do not discover this in a negotiation.**

## 7. The new slides — copy, verbatim

### SLIDE 4 — THE SHOW

> **Header:** THE SHOW · **Headline:** TWO SEASONS OF TELEVISION FROM ONE SEASON OF BASKETBALL

**8–10 episodes · two drops · one continuous story**

**PART ONE — THE BUILD** *(4–6 eps, released October, before the first game)*
The assembly. A celebrity alumnus returns to a program that has not mattered in twenty years and
is handed a top-ten budget. A GM with an NBA scouting background hunts the portal for players the
system overlooked. Coaches, families, agents, money. **Ends on the roster being finished — and the
first game unplayed.**

**PART TWO — THE SEASON** *(4–6 eps, released March, into the tournament)*
What the money bought. Chemistry, injury, pressure, a campus that has never carried expectation.
**Ends in March — on a result nobody involved can script.**

**Why the split works:** Part One turns the audience into stakeholders *before the outcome exists*.
They watch the season in real time having already chosen a side. **The show creates its own live
audience for the games Netflix is also carrying.**

### SLIDE 5 — EPISODE ONE

> **Header:** EPISODE ONE · **Headline:** THE COLD OPEN

**Minute one.** March 2008. Davidson, a 10-seed, beats Gonzaga, Georgetown and Wisconsin. Stephen
Curry, 20 years old, outscores entire teams. One basket from the Final Four.

**Minute three.** The same gym, empty. Seventeen seasons. No tournament wins. A coach explaining
that his best player left in April for a school that offered $800,000.

**Minute five.** Curry walks back into that gym — and this time he has a checkbook.

> **Pull quote across the bottom:**
> *"Everybody loves a Cinderella. Nobody noticed that we killed her."*

**The thesis in one sentence:** the tournament's best story has been quietly defunded, and this is
the first attempt to buy it back.

### SLIDE 11 — AUDIENCE

> **Header:** AUDIENCE · **Headline:** WHO SHOWS UP

**Three audiences that do not normally overlap:**

1. **College basketball — 68 teams, every region.** March Madness is the most geographically
   distributed audience in American sport. Every one of those schools has an alumni base that has
   been told for fifteen years that it cannot compete.
2. **The celebrity's audience**, which is not a sports audience. Curry's following is global and
   skews younger and more female than college basketball's.
3. **The underdog-sports-doc audience** — the *Wrexham* crossover, which demonstrated that the
   format travels to people with no prior interest in the sport or the league.

**The structural advantage:** a franchise in which **every season recruits a new regional fanbase**
— Charlotte, then Philadelphia, then Boston — and keeps the previous one.

⚠️ *Pull the current hours-viewed figures for Quarterback, Full Swing, Starting 5 and Wrexham
before the meeting and put them on this slide. Netflix thinks in hours viewed; speak their unit.*

### SLIDE 14 — THE FRANCHISE

> **Header:** FRANCHISE · **Headline:** THIS IS NOT ONE SHOW

**Season 1 (2027–28):** Davidson × Curry · a second school in parallel
**Season 2 (2028–29):** two more schools · women's basketball
**Season 3 (2029–30):** D3 reclassification (NYU) · D1 hockey (Rochester) · lacrosse (Johns Hopkins)

**Why it compounds rather than repeats:**
- **The format is the asset, not the school.** New campus, new celebrity, new region each season —
  the same engine
- **There are ~300 eligible programs.** The supply does not run out
- **Every season is a new regional audience** that keeps watching the old ones
- **It is counter-programmed to itself** — the tournament provides an annual, immovable finale date

> **One line, bottom of slide:** *Welcome to Wrexham found one club. There are three hundred.*

## 8. Two edits to slides you are keeping

**Slide 4 (Season 1) — delete the Apple TV / HBO Max / Netflix logo row.** Competitive tension is
created in conversation, not printed on a page you hand to one of the three. Showing Netflix their
competitors in your deck reads as leverage-seeking, and it is the one unforced error on an
otherwise excellent slide.

**Slide 9 (Comparisons) — answer the Last Chance U question directly.** The checklist is good but it
argues the wrong thing. Add one line:

> *Last Chance U filmed a program that had already lost. **We are financing one before it plays a
> game** — and the audience watches the bet resolve in real time.*

That is the distinction, it is true, and it is the thing Riegg will be weighing.

## 9. What I did not change, and why

**Slide 2 (Problem) is the best slide in the deck** — "Since the inception of NIL, 0 mid-majors or
low-majors have made the Sweet Sixteen. **Cinderella is dead.**" One number, one verdict, five faces
with price tags. Do not touch it.

**Slide 8 (Fan engagement) is a genuine differentiator** and reads better to Netflix than you may
think — it is an engagement-and-retention story, which is the metric they actually run on. Keep it.

**Slide 6 (Live games) is the Bajaria hook.** She spearheaded WWE Raw and NFL Christmas. A
documentary that arrives with live inventory attached is the rarest thing in that building.
