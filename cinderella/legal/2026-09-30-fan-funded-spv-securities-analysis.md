# Fan funding of an SPV — securities analysis, and the cleanest legal path

_2026-09-30. Prompted by Andrew Fried's suggestion (Boardwalk call #2, 09-29) that part of an SPV
be crowdfunded so fans hold a stake in the team's success._

---

## Bottom line, up front

1. **Any fan stake that carries an expectation of profit is a security.** No structure avoids that.
2. **A token changes nothing.** It is an unregistered security with extra regulatory problems.
3. **Three legitimate registered/exempt routes exist** (506(c), Reg CF, Reg A+), and each carries a
   cost most people underestimate: **public disclosure of the deal's economics.**
4. ⚠️ **The NCAA problem is bigger than the securities problem** and is the thing most likely to
   kill it.
5. ⭐ **The best version is not equity at all.** See §7. It is legally frictionless, uncapped in
   participants, and — because the real prize is *audience*, not capital — **strictly better on the
   merits**, not a compromise.

---

## 1. Is a fan stake a security? Yes.

*SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.* — an investment contract exists where there is (a) an investment of money,
(b) in a common enterprise, (c) with a reasonable **expectation of profits**, (d) derived from the
efforts of others. A fan paying money for a share of an SPV's upside satisfies all four without
argument. **It is a security, and selling it requires registration or an exemption.**

## 2. Tokenising it does not help — it makes it worse

The SEC has treated profit-participating tokens as securities consistently since the **DAO Report
(2017)**: *Munchee*, *Telegram*, *Kik*, *LBRY*, and on the NFT side *Impact Theory* and
*Stoner Cats*. Calling the instrument a token, a coin or an NFT does not change the Howey analysis.

**What tokenisation adds, all bad:** transfer-agent and custody questions · a secondary market that
likely requires a registered **broker-dealer or ATS** · possible money-transmitter licensing · state
money-transmission and virtual-currency regimes · and a materially worse posture with the NCAA and
with schools. **Do not pursue this. It is the most legally expensive version of the idea.**

## 3. The three real routes

| | Cap / 12 mo | Who can invest | Public marketing | Disclosure | Cost & time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Reg D 506(b)** | Unlimited | Accredited + up to 35 sophisticated non-accredited | ❌ No general solicitation | Light | Low / weeks |
| **Reg D 506(c)** | Unlimited | **Accredited only**, with verification | ✅ Yes | Light | Low / weeks |
| **Reg CF** (Title III) | **~$5M** | **Anyone**, with per-investor limits | ✅ Via a registered portal | Form C + reviewed or audited financials + annual reports | Moderate / 2–4 months |
| **Reg A+ Tier 2** | **$75M** | **Anyone** (10% limits for non-accredited) | ✅ Yes | **Form 1-A SEC qualification, audited financials, semi-annual reporting** | High — six figures / 4–8 months |

Also worth knowing: **Rule 147/147A intrastate** offerings. A single-state raise — e.g.
Massachusetts only, for Merrimack — avoids federal registration but is limited to one state's
residents and that state's own rules. Narrow, but a real option for a local-fan play.

⚠️ **Note the ceiling.** Reg CF caps at about **$5M**. Our SPV needs roughly **$3M** from a capital
partner, so Reg CF *could* technically cover it. But see §4 and §5 before concluding it should.

## 4. ⚠️ The NCAA problem — the one most likely to kill it

`Process/2026-06-24-ncaa-compliance-donor-collective-analysis.md` already records the governing
risk: **an SPV funded exclusively by one school's donors looks like a booster collective**, and the
stated fix is **mixed capital**.

**Crowdfunding Merrimack from Merrimack fans is, in substance, assembling a donor collective** — and
that money would fund athlete compensation. That is precisely the fact pattern the fair-market-value
test and NIL Go exist to police, and precisely what the Protect College Sports Act tightens
(`Process/2026-09-13-nil-sponsor-architecture-and-pcsa.md`).

**Mitigations, if equity crowdfunding proceeds anyway:**
- Raise at the **franchise / parent level, not the school SPV** — fans buy into *Making Cinderella*,
  not into Merrimack.
- **Cap any single school's fan concentration** in the vehicle.
- Keep the SPV **majority-funded by unaffiliated institutional capital**.
- Never let fan money be traceable to a specific athlete payment.

## 5. ⚠️ The disclosure problem — the objection Fried will not have considered

**Reg CF and Reg A+ both require public financial disclosure.** That means the following land in a
filing anyone can read, including every competitor and every counterparty:

- the school revenue-share terms and the trailing baseline
- talent compensation and attachment economics
- **athlete NIL amounts**
- sponsor pricing
- Cinderella Corp's franchise fee and management fee

**Schools will refuse. Talent will refuse. Sponsors will refuse.** In practice this is the
dealbreaker for equity crowdfunding, independent of the securities work.

**The non-security route in §7 has no disclosure requirement at all.**

## 6. Structural traps if fans hold real equity

- **Use a single feeder vehicle.** All crowdfunded investors into one entity that holds **one**
  interest in the SPV. Thousands of direct members destroys the ROFR, co-sale and drag-along
  mechanics in the Stockholders' Agreement.
- **K-1s.** A pass-through feeder issues a K-1 to every investor annually — operationally brutal at
  scale. A **C-corp feeder** avoids this (at the cost of a tax layer).
- **Investment Company Act.** §3(c)(1) caps a private fund at 100 beneficial owners. The SPV is an
  operating business rather than a securities holder, so the Act likely does not apply — **but
  counsel must confirm**, because the feeder looks more fund-like than the SPV does.
- ⚠️ **Side Letter interaction.** SPV cash reaching Cinderella Corp as distributions or fees is
  **Gross Revenue** under the seed Side Letter — so **50% of it goes to seed investors until 1×.**
  Any fan-funding design must be modelled against that waterfall, not assumed to be free money.

## 7. ⭐⭐ The most legally friendly version — and the better one

**Two tiers. The first is not a security at all.**

### Tier 1 — the "Founders Club" (unlimited participants, no securities law)

$50–$500. What a member gets:

- a numbered certificate and their **name on the floor / in the arena / in the credits**
- a **vote on cosmetic decisions** — jersey design, walk-out song, a charity, a captain's patch
- behind-the-scenes content, a members' channel, merch, early ticket access
- standing as a founding member of the story

What a member explicitly does **not** get: **no dividends, no revenue share, no appreciation, no
resale right, no claim on assets.**

**Why this is not a security:** with no expectation of profit, it **fails the third Howey prong.**
This is the **Green Bay Packers** structure — shares that famously confer pride and nothing
economic, and are marketed as *not an investment*. ⚠️ **Counsel must confirm**, and the marketing
must never imply financial return — that language is what creates a security, not the instrument.

**Consequences:** no SEC filing · no accreditation · no disclosure of deal economics · no investor
caps · no K-1s · no NCAA collective characterisation from an equity stake · and the money is
**revenue**, not capital.

### Tier 2 — accredited fans, via Reg D 506(c)

For the subset who want genuine economics. Public marketing is permitted, accreditation must be
verified, disclosure stays light. Into a single feeder vehicle per §6.

### ⭐ Why Tier 1 is better rather than a lesser option

**The value of fan funding is the audience, not the capital.**

50,000 people at $100 is **$5M and 50,000 emotionally committed viewers** — which is exactly the
asset a streamer is buying, and exactly the "GameStop" dynamic Fried described. Reg CF would raise
the same $5M ceiling while adding SEC filings, public disclosure of athlete pay, and a booster-
collective argument.

**So optimise for participant count, not dollars raised.** On that objective the non-security route
wins outright. It is not the cautious version of Fried's idea — **it is the version that works.**

## 8. What to tell Fried

The idea is good and the mechanic is achievable — **just not as equity.** Frame it as: *fans buy
in for standing and voice, not for return; we keep the capital stack institutional.* He gets the
community flywheel he is describing, we avoid SEC registration, public disclosure of athlete
compensation, and an NCAA fight we do not need.

## 9. For counsel

1. Confirm a **non-economic Founders Club membership is outside the definition of a security**, and
   review all marketing copy for anything implying return.
2. Confirm the **Investment Company Act** does not reach a feeder holding a single SPV interest.
3. Confirm **no NCAA/NIL characterisation risk** attaches to non-economic fan memberships, and
   advise on franchise-level vs. school-level solicitation.
4. Model **Founders Club proceeds against the Side Letter Gross Revenue waterfall.**
5. If equity crowdfunding is pursued regardless: **Reg CF vs. Reg A+ vs. intrastate**, and whether
   schools and talent will consent to the required disclosure.
