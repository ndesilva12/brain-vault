# NIL / Sponsor Architecture — and the Protect College Sports Act

_2026-09-13. How player compensation gets justified under the fair-market-value test, why the
sponsor relationship is the engine that makes it work, and where the pending federal legislation
sits. Supersedes nothing; read alongside
`Process/2026-06-24-ncaa-compliance-donor-collective-analysis.md`, which predates the bill and
does not reference it._

## 1. The legislation — Protect College Sports Act of 2026 (S.4668 / H.R.9137)

**Status as of 2026-09-13.** Cruz (R-TX) and Cantwell (D-WA), with Schmitt (R-MO) and Coons
(D-DE). Cleared Senate Commerce **19–9**. Cloture motion on the motion to proceed filed by Thune
on Aug 5; the chamber adjourned without voting. **Cloture vote scheduled no earlier than Sept 15
and no later than Sept 23, 2026.** Cruz lobbied for it on College GameDay at Texas on Sept 12 and
was booed. The House has not acted.

**Odds: genuinely uncertain.** Big Ten and SEC endorsed in early August; nearly every conference
and the HBCUs support it; Trump has signalled he would sign. Against that: the Congressional Black
Caucus and AFL-CIO oppose, Booker and Murphy frame it as a civil-rights issue, and Republicans
have raised late objections over preemption of state laws on transgender athletes in women's
sports. Failure to obtain unanimous consent before recess was a bad signal. **Even in the good
case, House action pushes final passage toward year-end or 2027.** Cruz's "Trump signs it on
GameDay" is aspiration, not forecast.

### Provisions that matter to MC

| | |
|---|---|
| **Third-party NIL test** | Deals need a **valid business purpose** and compensation **"commensurate with compensation paid to individuals with a similar profile, reputation, or notability who are not student athletes."** Codifies the House settlement standard already enforced through NIL Go |
| **Disclosure** | Anything over **$600 in 12 months**, within **5 days** |
| **Antitrust immunity** | NCAA, conferences and schools exempt when enforcing compensation, transfer, eligibility and recruitment rules |
| **Preemption** | Overrides state NIL / transfer / eligibility law — one national rulebook. **Materially good for a multi-state franchise** |
| **Institutional cap** | Rises from ~$20.5M to **$48.8M**, plus a new **$22.5M retention fund** (→$27.5M with women's/Olympic NIL investment) |
| **Transfers** | One free; a second requires sitting out a year. **Helps roster continuity** |
| **Employment** | Athletes barred from employee status |
| **Media rights** | Conferences may pool **across** conferences under a Sports Broadcasting Act extension — a pool of pools. ⚠️ May affect whether a school still controls the non-conference inventory MC plans to package as live events |

### Corrections to earlier analysis in this session

- ❌ *"The cap raise makes MC money non-additive."* **Wrong for our fact pattern.** The cap binds
  institutions, and **our schools are nowhere near it** — Davidson will not spend $20.5M, let alone
  $48.8M. The cap constrains Texas, not us.
- ❌ *"The third-party loophole closing targets MC."* **Wrong.** What is being closed is schools
  routing money through **their own** multimedia-rights partners, sponsors and apparel brands —
  school-adjacent money in third-party costume. MC is a genuinely independent counterparty buying
  a real service.
- ❌ *"Our players are unknowns in Season 1."* **Wrong.** The concept targets players who were
  overlooked **originally**, proved themselves over a season or two at a low level, and now hold
  real offers. At the moment of payment they are demonstrably valuable, with genuine notability,
  production and following.
- ✅ **What the bill actually does to us** is raise the *price of the player* without raising the
  *defensible ceiling*. See §2.

## 2. The scissors problem

Two lines move in opposite directions:

- **What we must pay to land a player rises** with the institutional cap ($20.5M → $48.8M) and
  with the new $22.5M retention fund — which is purpose-built to persuade proven players not to
  transfer. That is our exact mechanic, at our exact moment, better funded than we are.
- **What we can defend as fair market value does not move**, because the test benchmarks against
  **non-student-athletes**. A competing school's offer is the one comparable we can never cite —
  it is another athlete's compensation, which is precisely the athletic value the test exists to
  exclude.

**Target band.** We are pursuing the very best players in the country. If the top earner makes
~$7M we likely cannot reach him; the workable tranche is roughly the **next 10–30 highest earners
nationally**, in the ~$2–3M range. If that tranche moves to ~$5M, the amount we must bring in from
sponsors has to move with it.

## 3. The architecture that answers it

**Docuseries participation alone is a weak FMV anchor** — unscripted talent fees are thin and,
critically, **no public comp table exists** (searched 2026-09-13: athlete docuseries participation
fees are not disclosed for *Quarterback*, *Full Swing*, *Drive to Survive*, or Alix Earle's Netflix
series). Real commercial work is a strong anchor — brand campaigns have deep, liquid, non-athlete
comparables.

### ⭐ The key insight

**Let the sponsor contract directly with the player. Do not take a cut, and do not set the price.**

An arm's-length deal between an independent sponsor and the athlete **is** fair market value by
definition — willing buyer, willing seller, no common interest. That is the strongest evidence
obtainable under the test, and it cannot be manufactured. **The moment MC sets the number or takes
a cut, MC owns the valuation risk.** Standing outside the transaction, we do not.

### What MC sells — three things we actually own

1. **On-screen integration and Official Partner billing** — media inventory
2. **Category exclusivity inside the show's ecosystem** — no competing brand gets in
3. **Access** — first-look or introduction rights to the roster

Sponsor pays MC for those. Sponsor pays players separately and directly for their own work. Two
clean transactions, no cut, no agency.

### ⭐ The negative covenant — what creates the auction

Access alone is not scarce; any brand can approach any player. **Category exclusivity is what
makes sponsors bid against each other.**

**Put a negative covenant in the player participation agreements:** during the season, the player
will not sign a competing brand in the Official Partner's category. Standard practice in
professional sports — team sponsors hold category exclusivity and individual player deals are
restricted accordingly. The SPV compensates for the restriction as part of the participation fee.

That converts *"you can talk to our players"* into *"you are the only one who can"* — which is the
thing brands actually bid for. Without it, Official Partner status is just a logo.

⚠️ **Counsel check:** the covenant restricts an athlete's NIL. Routine in pro sports, but the bill
creates a **federal NIL right with a private right of action**. Keep it narrow — one category,
in-season only, tied to identifiable compensation.

### The fallback if the covenant doesn't survive

**We still win.** Even with no exclusivity, sponsors want players who sit on a large platform —
ours. Their direct deals **raise what a player earns by choosing our school over another**, funded
by someone other than us. That is a recruiting advantage and a roster-cost subsidy in one, and it
survives whatever happens to the covenant.

### The trade worth making

Our main economic line is **team revenue** — the incremental school share over five years.
Sponsorship is partly a means to it: it makes the roster affordable, the team wins, and the revenue
we share in grows.

**Therefore: deliberately leave money on the sponsorship table in exchange for the sponsor
committing to larger direct player deals.** Those fund the roster at no cost to us, produce the
cleanest FMV evidence available, and deliver the same on-court result. Probably the most efficient
trade in the model.

### ⚠️ The trap — do not become an agent

If MC negotiates NIL deals **on behalf of** athletes for compensation, it may be acting as an
agent. Under the bill that means registration **and a 5% fee cap** — which would be catastrophic
for SPV economics.

**Be a principal, not an intermediary.** Either the SPV acquires NIL rights for its own account and
packages what it owns, or the sponsor contracts directly with each athlete and MC is purely the
producer. Never broker someone else's deal for a fee. **Structure this deliberately and early — it
is hard to unwind.**

## 4. Consequences for other parts of the model

- **Sunjay's Track A (lead/presenting sponsor) is the compliance engine, not just a revenue line.**
  The sponsor is what makes the player payments defensible. Raises the strategic priority of that
  deal considerably.
- **"Top-10 budget nationally" needs recalibrating.** Against a $48.8M cap plus a $22.5M retention
  fund, $10–12M is not a top-10 budget. *"Best-funded team in their conference by a wide margin"*
  is defensible, verifiable, and still sells. Fix this in the deck before an investor or buyer does
  the arithmetic.
- **Roster allocation.** A sponsor campaign features three or four players, not thirteen. Stars
  carry large defensible numbers on commercial work; the back of the roster still needs a separate
  justification.
- **Documentation is operational, not legal.** Ten commercials contracted and two delivered is the
  exact abuse the test hunts. Real shoots, real air time, contemporaneous records.

## 5. Open questions for counsel

1. Does the category-exclusivity covenant survive the bill's federal NIL right and private right of
   action? How narrow must it be?
2. Principal vs. agent — confirm the SPV structure that avoids agent registration and the 5% cap.
3. Under the cross-conference media pooling extension, does a participating school still control
   its non-conference inventory for separate licensing to MC?
4. Does anything in the bill reach a genuinely independent third party like MC, as distinct from
   school-affiliated sponsors and multimedia-rights partners?
